Part 1 · Filters and edges

Convolution makes local change visible.

I used zero-filled, same-size padding throughout. Difference filters expose intensity changes; Gaussian smoothing lets the meaningful structure survive before thresholding.

1.2

Finite differences

I convolved the cameraman image with Dx and Dy, computed √(Dx2 + Dy2), then selected a threshold that keeps major outlines while suppressing most texture noise.

The raw magnitude includes fine-grained background variation. Blurring before differentiation reduces those high-frequency responses, so its lower threshold produces more coherent contours around the photographer and camera.

1.3

Derivative of Gaussian

I built a 25 × 25 Gaussian from OpenCV’s 1D Gaussian kernel and outer product, then convolved it with Dx and Dy. This combines smoothing and differentiation in one filter.

The one-pass DoG response matches the sequential blurred-then-differentiated result to 2.257 × 10−15 in the interior. The visible boundary discrepancy is expected: applying two zero-padded operations creates a wider affected border. For the orientation visualization, I used arctan(Dy / Dx) with explicit quadrant corrections rather than a built-in angle function.

Part 2.1 · Applications

Sharpening is a controlled high-frequency boost.

Unsharp masking subtracts a Gaussian-blurred image from the original to isolate high frequencies, then adds a scaled version of that detail back.

Isharp=I + α(I − G * I)α controls the amount

Increasing the amount makes fine lines and contrast more apparent, but it can also create halos and amplify noise. Sharpening the deliberately blurred image recovers some edge contrast, but not the original detail: the lost high frequencies cannot be perfectly reconstructed.

Part 2.2 · Hybrid images

Distance changes the image you see.

Each hybrid combines the low frequencies of one aligned image with the high frequencies of another. Up close the high-frequency subject dominates; at a distance the low-frequency subject takes over.

Alignment is essential: matching eyes, nose, and overall scale allows the two interpretations to share a coherent spatial structure. I kept color in both components; it preserves recognizable low-frequency color regions while retaining crisp, colorful high-frequency details.

Part 2.4 · Multiresolution blending

Blend each frequency band with a soft mask.

I formed Laplacian stacks for both images and a Gaussian stack for the mask, blended corresponding levels, and summed the result. This makes a seam gradual at low frequencies while retaining sharp detail at high frequencies.

The irregular butterfly mask is where multiresolution blending is most useful: a hard cut would expose an obvious pasted boundary, but a blurred mask smoothly transfers broad color while allowing the butterfly’s high-frequency edges to remain defined.

Takeaway

What I learned

Filtering is not just a cleanup step. It is a way to choose which visual information gets to matter: differences isolate edges, low and high passes change perception, and frequency bands let two images meet without a visible seam.
